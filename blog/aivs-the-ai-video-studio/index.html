aivs - the ai video studio

October 25, 2026 · Updated October 26, 2026 · Andrew Arrow

Sue and Joe are stranded beside a station wagon on a desert road. Joe emerges from behind the engine. “Good news. It’s definitely the car.” That’s enough for me to understand the scene. To film it, somebody still has to decide where Joe is standing, how he gets into view, and whether the camera can see him do it. Leaving those decisions to the video model can produce a man walking through a car.

AIVS is the AI video studio I’m building around that gap. You write or import a screenplay, choose a scene, and cast its roles. Codex turns that scene into a shooting script with timed directions for the action, camera, dialogue, and sound. LTX-2.5 then generates picture and speech together in short takes on an Apple Silicon Mac. The desktop app is Rust; Python runs the planning and video pipeline. The story stays readable while the shooting script handles the details needed to film it.

Import the script, film one scene

The story can start in another editor. Fountain is an open screenplay format that keeps the script in readable plain text. The nyousefi/Fountain repository provides an open source implementation and a full-length Big Fish screenplay as an example. Open that file and you can read the scenes and dialogue directly, without a screenwriting app.

The new import command brings those files into AIVS through File → Import…. Scene headings, action, character cues, parentheticals, dialogue, and transitions become editable elements. Import replaces the current screenplay and clears its casting; Undo restores both. The original .fountain file stays untouched, and the imported document autosaves to screenplay.json. The importer leaves out title-page metadata, outline sections, notes, omitted passages, page breaks, and emphasis formatting. Simultaneous dialogue becomes dialogue in source order.

You can keep the full screenplay in the editor and work on one scene at a time. The scene selector above Generate video lists the scene headings. Choose one and cast its roles; AIVS sends only that scene and its cast to the shooting-script pipeline. Uncast roles elsewhere in the script don’t block it. The 100,000-character generation limit applies to the selected scene, so a long document no longer has to be cut into separate files just to render a scene from it.

Sue is a role. Ava can play her.

The script originally called its two people Ava and Eli, the same names as their saved character profiles. The rename to Sue and Joe made the distinction explicit. Ava belongs in the library of performers; Sue belongs in the story. Recasting Sue shouldn’t require hunting through the script to replace every name.

Now you can drag Ava’s portrait or name onto any of Sue’s dialogue blocks. Every Sue cue gets the same assignment. Drop Eli onto Joe, and both parts are cast. The Uncast / played by control beside a character cue also opens a picker, including an option to clear the assignment. Selecting a portrait in the gallery just browses it; casting requires choosing a role. This diagram shows the assignments in an earlier, five-line version of the scene:

The implementation stores one assignment per role, rather than one per line. The screenplay editor and generation request come from the same document. Every role in the selected scene must be cast before Generate video becomes available.

The old request embedded performer references in a long prompt. Rust now sends the screenplay text and structured cast bindings to Python. The cast portion looks like this:

"cast": [
  { "role": "JOE", "character": "eli" },
  { "role": "SUE", "character": "ava" }
]

Python loads those saved profiles explicitly. A role called Ava can’t accidentally cast itself just by sharing a library name. The scene’s dialogue keeps its role names, and the renderer adds appearance and voice descriptions under those names. You can edit the screenplay and add scenes in the GUI; text and cast assignments autosave together in the workspace.

The screenplay needs a shooting script

The screenplay describes what happens: the setting, actions, characters, and dialogue. It can leave quite a lot to the people making the film. The updated README pairs a short desert-road screenplay with the shooting script produced for it. The source has three spoken lines, ending with Joe’s “He was an optimistic man.” One action sentence reads:

SUE, 30s, turns a battered postcard over in her hands. JOE, late 20s, emerges from behind the engine.

Codex gets that screenplay, the cast descriptions, ordered story beats, and director instructions. A beat is one source action or dialogue line. Its job is to divide those beats into timed shots and work out how to show them. In the saved first shot, the short entrance becomes this:

SUE stands on the gravel screen left beside the front passenger door, holding a battered postcard in both hands. JOE stands on the ground screen right alongside the engine bay, empty hands clear of the engine, upper body partly screened by the raised hood. His feet and route along the near fender remain visible.

Now Joe has somewhere to stand and a visible route into the shot. The rest of the prompt directs his step, eyeline, and line delivery, then Sue’s almost-smile. It even specifies what fills the quiet ending: “dry wind, faint cooling-engine ticks, and gravel crunch synchronized with JOE’s step.” That’s the work the shooting script adds. The writer can keep “emerges from behind the engine” in the screenplay.

The saved screenplay and full shooting script show two shots totaling 21 seconds:

ShotLengthSource beatsAction and dialogue
110 seconds1–4Establish the wagon and postcard; Joe steps into view and delivers his first line; Sue almost smiles and looks down the road.
211 seconds5–6Sue delivers her line about the ocean; Joe looks around and replies; finish on a quiet reaction.

This is a saved planning example, so 21 seconds isn’t a promise about every render or evidence that the performance worked. Its second shot also calls for tighter framing. The current director instructions require every later shot within a scene to inherit the preceding shot’s actual last frame. Reframing has to develop through a continuous camera move from that composition. A new scene resets the frame reference. Carrying the frame forward helps preserve faces, clothes, positions, and props; it doesn’t lock identity across locations.

Codex chooses staging, but Python checks the proposed plan before filming it. Every source beat must appear exactly once, in order, and a take can’t cross scene boundaries. Dialogue enters the template through placeholders such as {{line_3}}; Python fills in the original speaker, delivery cue, and words. It rejects duplicated placeholders, raw quoted dialogue, insufficient duration, and prompts over 1,024 Gemma tokens. A validation failure gets one corrective Codex attempt before the job fails. The run saves both a readable shooting-script.txt and a machine-readable shooting-script.json.

The renderer generates those four-to-twelve-second takes in sequence and joins them with FFmpeg, which also supplies the final fade. Planning through Codex uses the configured service and login; video inference and speech verification run locally. Keeping the words intact in the plan still leaves the model’s performance to check.

Joe needs somewhere to stand

An earlier render report described Joe emerging from the car without opening a door, plus gibberish at the end of the first shot. A valid shot list hadn’t made the performance physically sensible. Here’s a frame six seconds into the first take of an older, three-shot, 27-second plan for the longer Last Light scene. Even the requested red-rock desert has become a tree-lined road.

The director instructions now make Codex account for the opening frame and each movement that follows. Joe is already on the ground beside the raised hood, with the cabin doors shut. He steps around the fender into view. If a scene really requires an exit through a closed door, the plan has to include opening it. The longer scene’s postcard handoff needed the same care: Sue gives it to Joe, but she pockets it later, so somebody has to give it back.

The soundtrack also needs an ending. Each dialogue take now asks for the speaker’s lips to settle after the last word, with the remaining action accompanied by environmental sound. Silent takes get explicit direction too. These instructions live in a director-instructions file, where they can improve staging across scripts without adding Python rules about particular cars or postcards.

One easy mistake is to put the important detail only in start_state or end_state. Those fields are continuity notes for review. The model receives the assembled prompt, so its opening positions, prop ownership, and quiet ending have to be written there. A carried last frame can help preserve a composition; it can’t supply a missing physical cause.

Planning is visible now, too. The GUI shows elapsed time and streams Codex activity while the shooting script is being prepared. The run keeps the requests, responses, and streamed diagnostics, including partial output when planning stops or times out. A long planning turn no longer has to look like a frozen Generate button.

Keeping the performer when the location changes

Better staging still leaves a casting problem. The normal screenplay path supplies appearance and voice descriptions, not each performer’s saved portrait and recorded voice. Continuation can carry a face into the next shot, but a new location needs a way to establish it again. Text describing auburn hair and freckles doesn’t uniquely identify Ava.

The character-consistency work added a separate command-line experiment: Lena plays Sue and Owen plays Joe, first on a desert road, then in a café. Each shot has one visible speaker. The prepared starting images use the original character portraits as references. These two images are inputs for Lena’s takes, with their generation prompts and source references saved alongside them:

These images condition complete starting frames. That’s why a solo gallery portrait is awkward for an ensemble scene: it also tells the model to fill the frame with one face. The experiment prepares a composition for each take and changes the camera separately from the performer’s original identity reference.

It also makes the dialogue before the video. A CPU speech worker uses Lena’s saved Kokoro voice ID, af_sarah, and Owen’s am_michael. Each source line is synthesized once, measured, and reused in both locations. The shot soundtrack adds a short opening pause and silence after the line. This gives the video a fixed vocal performance to follow instead of asking it to invent a new voice for every take.

The audio-driven backend accepts that soundtrack and the starting image together. Final encoding selects the supplied soundtrack rather than the model’s generated audio. The preparation records image and audio hashes, voice and model versions, measured durations, and speaker intervals. Saved-plan freshness checks now include the character reference-asset hashes as well, so replacing an asset can invalidate a cached plan even if its description hasn’t changed.

This is still an opt-in experiment, separate from GUI screenplay generation. The four-shot test uses 768 × 448 at 24 fps. The inspected run has one completed four-second take, marked rendered-needs-review; the comparison across both locations remains incomplete. Faces, voice identity, spoken words, and lip synchronization still need review. A supplied voice track doesn’t by itself tell a model which of two visible faces should be speaking.

A playable file still needs a director

The normal GUI path uses LTX-2.5 to generate picture and speech together at 1280 × 704 and 24 fps. Video inference runs locally from cached models; shooting-script preparation uses the signed-in Codex client. Initial model setup still downloads about 43 GB and requires 50 GiB free. Rust owns process lifetime, including Stop, and completed films play inside the native window through macOS AVKit.

Before continuing to the next take, Whisper transcribes the generated audio locally, without seeing the expected script. The app compares the recognized words with the source dialogue. Missing, substituted, repeated, or extra recognized words stop the run, leaving completed takes and transcript reports available to inspect. The assembled film must pass speech, decoded-frame, and audio/video timing checks before it becomes video.mp4. Whisper can mishear a valid line or miss an unintelligible sound; matching words still don’t prove the right person spoke them or that the lips line up.

I can now fix how Joe enters the shot without changing his dialogue or filling the screenplay with camera instructions. The shooting script makes those decisions explicit enough to inspect before rendering, and the saved takes let me compare the plan with what happened on screen. Faces, voices, and physical actions still need a director’s eye. But when Joe walks through the car, I have a specific entrance to fix.
